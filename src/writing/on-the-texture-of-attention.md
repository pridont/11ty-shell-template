We tend to imagine the moral life beginning at the moment of decision — the fork in the
road, the deliberation, the choice. But by the time we are choosing, most of the work is
already done. What we are able to choose between depends on what we have managed to notice.

Attention is selective by necessity. The world offers far more than we can hold, and so we
are always, quietly, deciding what counts as salient. These decisions are rarely conscious;
they are shaped by habit, by training, by the company we keep — and they settle, over years,
into something like character.

{% figure "/assets/images/treescape.jpg", "Fog lying in the valleys between forested ridges, with treetops emerging above it.", "Figure 1. What the fog hides is not gone; it is simply not yet salient." %}

This is why I think perception belongs at the center of ethics rather than its periphery. To
become a better person is, in large part, to become a better noticer: to see the tired
colleague, the excluded student, the small cruelty we would rather overlook.

None of this is fast, and none of it is heroic. It is the slow, unglamorous work of learning
to look — and then looking again.
