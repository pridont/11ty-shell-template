---
title: Against Frictionless Choice
date: 2025-09-08
excerpt: On the moral value of difficulty, and what smooth interfaces take from us.
---

The design ideal of the last two decades has been the removal of friction: fewer steps, less
hesitation, no gap between wanting and having. It is a good ideal for most things, and a
quietly corrosive one for a few.

Difficulty is not merely a cost. A pause is where a preference gets examined, where a
first impulse is allowed to be revised, where the person we are at three in the morning is
kept at some distance from the person who has to live with the result.

Removing friction from a decision does not make the decision easier. It makes it smaller —
and a life composed of very small decisions is not obviously one anybody chose.

## Where the pause still works

A few frictions are worth defending on purpose rather than losing by accident:

- The walk to the shop, instead of the one-tap delivery
- The night you sleep on an email before sending it
- The paper form that makes you write the reason down

None of these are *efficient*. That is rather the point — each one buys a small, deliberate
delay between the wanting and the having, and the delay is where the better self gets a vote.

> The hurried man is a lost man, however good the road.

That the frictionless version is usually *possible* now does not mean it is owed to us. Some
doors are worth leaving stiff.

---

We do not need to romanticize inconvenience to notice this. We only need to admit that
**speed and ease are not neutral** — they are a thumb on the scale, and it is worth asking,
each time, which way the scale is being pushed.
